On **26 September 2026**, the Youth Alliance for Agroecology and Climate held its fifth YAAC Talk, online and streamed live on LinkedIn, Facebook and YouTube. The theme: *"Feeding without destroying: agroecology and Africa's triple challenge — climate, soils and food sovereignty."*

The starting question was easy to ask and hard to settle: **how do we feed Africa without destroying the resources its future depends on?**

## Two perspectives, from the lab to the field

The session brought together:

- **Dr Tatiana Krasova Wade**, scientific project officer at IRD, within the Eco&Sol joint research unit, based at the Joint Microbiology Laboratory (LCM) in Dakar;
- **Dr Alexandre Zoumman**, president of YAAC, soil microbiologist and agroecologist.

The third announced panellist, Mr Zimare Issifou, an agronomist, had to withdraw the day before because of a professional commitment. The session was moderated by **Donan Ahouangnimon**, a YAAC member and journalist by training.

## A science, a practice, a movement

Dr Wade opened by defining agroecology as at once a **nature-based farming practice**, an **interdisciplinary science** and a **social and political movement**. It does not try to dominate the environment with chemicals; it works with ecosystems: biodiversity, soil health, fewer synthetic inputs, better water management.

Dr Zoumman spelled out what it is not: *"Agroecology is not just the absence of chemicals."* It is a whole set of techniques and methods, from crop diversification and biomass recycling to agroforestry.

## Soils running out of breath

Dr Wade's assessment was stark. According to the figures she presented, **65% of Africa's farmland** has undergone some form of degradation since the mid-twentieth century, 20% of it severely. Soils can be restored, but it takes **3 to 50 years** depending on their condition. And the real question is time: can farmers afford to wait?

## Can agroecology feed Africa?

Both panellists said yes, with conditions.

Dr Zoumman called for honesty. The transition brings a **drop in yields for two to three years**, from 5–8% and sometimes up to 25% depending on the cropping system, before yields stabilise and then recover. That is the period many decision-makers, focused on immediate food security, are reluctant to accept.

He also reframed the problem: in Africa, a large share of what is grown is lost between the field and the market. *"The big problem is first a problem of storing food, not necessarily production itself."*

Dr Wade pointed out that smallholder systems, often agroecological without the label, **already feed around 70% of the population**, and withstand climate shocks better than intensive farming. What is missing, in her view, is the data and tooling to scale up: suitable mechanisation, supply chains that can handle intercropped harvests, and mass production of organic fertiliser.

## Farmers' knowledge and research: building together

Is agroecology a step backwards? For Dr Zoumman, it is rooted in what our parents did, but that knowledge, proven over centuries, now meets a climate that has changed. Hence the need to **co-design research projects with farmers**, who are experimenters themselves, so that their knowledge can be validated, standardised and shared to suit each country's conditions. Soil microbiology plays a central part in that work.

## Is money the real barrier?

Who should pay for the transition? Everyone, said Dr Wade: producers, consumers, civil society, governments and financial partners. She cited several continental initiatives already under way.

Dr Zoumman highlighted the gap between pledges and disbursements, and how rarely funding reaches smallholders. He argued for **compensation schemes** for farmers who absorb the transition's yield losses, for subsidies that stop favouring industrial inputs alone, and for a local industry producing **biofertilisers and biopesticides**.

## Looking ahead to 2050

Dr Wade described widespread conservation agriculture, producers recognised and paid for the environmental services they provide, local processing and a genuine continental market. Her closing message to young people: act *"not in 2050, but starting now."*

Dr Zoumman wants soils treated *"as living capital"*, biological inputs made locally by youth-led businesses, and farming restored as **a dignified profession and a source of pride**.
